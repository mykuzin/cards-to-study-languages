Methodology research

Build Kartky on open science, not brands

Which proven, license- and patent-free methods a topic-based vocabulary app can rest on, which open data it can ship, where the legal traps are, and what Kartky should build first.

Key findings

  • The methods Free for anyone to use

    Spaced repetition, retrieval practice, Leitner boxes, topic grouping and CEFR levels are unprotectable methods under Ukrainian, EU and US law. No live patent covers basic spaced-repetition flashcards.

  • Biggest gap No spaced retrieval yet

    Spacing has a medium-to-large effect in L2 learning, and repeated testing roughly doubled one-week retention in a classic study. Kartky’s known/learning marks never bring a word back.

  • Scheduler FSRS via ts-fsrs (MIT)

    The most accurate open scheduler in a benchmark of ~350M Anki reviews. Loads with a plain <script> tag; default parameters work without any per-user data.

  • Topic decks Frequency picks, topics group

    Drilling close relatives (mother, father, aunt, uncle) together causes confusion errors. Build decks around scenarios, choose words by frequency, and keep near-neighbours apart.

  • Legal risk It sits in content, not method

    A 15-year database right in the EU and Ukraine makes bulk-copying Oxford 3000, the English Vocabulary Profile or Cervantes inventories risky. Non-commercial and GPL data are the other traps.

  • Open data A source for every field

    CEFR-J and NGSL for word lists, wordfreq for frequency, Tatoeba for sentences, Open English WordNet for definitions, Twemoji for images, the browser’s speech engine for audio.

01Executive summary

Yes. Every method a vocabulary flashcard app needs is free to use. That covers spaced repetition, retrieval practice, two-way recall, topic grouping and CEFR-style levelling. Ukrainian, US and EU law all state outright that methods, procedures and systems are not protected by copyright, and patent law in Ukraine and at the European Patent Office excludes methods of intellectual activity “as such”.

The one early broad US patent on adaptive spaced repetition, Cerego’s US 6,652,283, expired in 2019 (zakon.rada.gov.ua 2811-IX; Google Patents). The method with the strongest evidence is also the one Kartky is missing: actively recalling words and reviewing them at spaced intervals. An L2 meta-analysis of 48 experiments found spacing has a medium-to-large effect, and in one classic study repeated testing roughly doubled one-week retention compared with re-studying (Western University; Purdue).

The best open scheduler is FSRS, available as the MIT-licensed ts-fsrs library. It runs in the browser with no build step and leads an open benchmark of hundreds of millions of Anki reviews (srs-benchmark). Topic-based study is defensible, with one condition. Drilling close co-hyponyms together produces confusion errors, so Kartky should let frequency choose the words, let the topic group them around scenarios, and shuffle sessions so near-neighbours never sit side by side (Nakata & Suzuki 2019).

The legal risk sits in content, not in method: copying Oxford 3000, the English Vocabulary Profile or the Instituto Cervantes inventories; using non-commercial or GPL datasets; bundling Anki’s AGPL code; or claiming “official CEFR alignment”. Open alternatives exist for every card field.

02No one can own spaced repetition or a topic deck

The legal starting point decides what “open methodology” can mean. Ukraine’s 2022 copyright law (No. 2811-IX, in force since 1 January 2023) says protection “does not extend to … ideas, theories, principles, methods, procedures, processes, systems … even if they are expressed, described or illustrated in a work” (Art. 7(3)).

The same law excludes from copyright the ideas and principles behind any part of a computer program, “including its interface, logic, algorithms” (Art. 20(3)) (zakon.rada.gov.ua 2811-IX). US law says the same in 17 U.S.C. §102(b) (LII), and Baker v. Selden (1879) held that a book describing a bookkeeping system gives no exclusive right to the system itself (Justia). In the EU, the Software Directive and the CJEU’s SAS Institute ruling protect neither a program’s functionality nor its interface ideas (EUR-Lex 2009/24/EC; CURIA C-406/10).

A flip card, an Again/Good button pair, a Leitner box, a spaced-review queue and a topic × level grid are all free to build.

Patents: the method is open, a few features are fenced

Ukraine’s invention law excludes, when claimed “as such”, “schemes, rules and methods” of intellectual activity, computer programs, and “forms of presenting information” (zakon.rada.gov.ua 3687-XII). EPC Article 52 has a parallel exclusion (EPO). In the US, Alice v. CLS Bank makes abstract ideas implemented on a generic computer ineligible (Justia).

A Google Patents review of the sector finds no live patent on generic spaced-repetition flashcards. Cerego’s 1999 patent on adaptive review scheduling is “Expired – Lifetime”, and Microsoft’s generic “adaptively-spaced repetition” application was abandoned without a grant (US6652283B1; US20120322043A1). What does exist is narrow and implementation-specific:

  • Rosetta Stone: speech and pronunciation-scoring patents (Google Patents).
  • Quizlet: ML grading, distractor generation and image suggestion, plus a pending application on “automatic selection of term study direction” (Google Patents).
  • Fluent Forever: an active patent, running to 2040, on a server-and-database flashcard structure with picture recommendation (US11288976B2).
  • Duolingo published its spaced-repetition model as an ACL paper with MIT-licensed code instead of patenting it (Settles & Meeder 2016; GitHub).

A plain spaced-repetition flashcard site sits on decades of prior art: Pimsleur in 1967, Leitner in 1972, and SuperMemo’s first computer implementation in 1987 (SuperMemo). Patent density rises only when an app adds pronunciation scoring, ML-generated questions, adaptive placement tests, or automated image or direction recommendation.

Not a freedom-to-operate opinion. Formal opinions typically cost USD 10,000 or more and lone developers rarely commission them (IPWatchdog; UK Business Forums). A self-search on Google Patents before building any of the riskier features above is the proportionate step.

Named methods and the CEFR come with conditions

SuperMemo World approves use of SM-2 in other software “along with the copyright note” and keeps later algorithms (SM-17 and up) as trade secrets (SuperMemo). The exact wording of the note could not be confirmed on an official page. Anki’s code is AGPL-3.0-or-later (Anki LICENSE), so copying its source would bring network source-disclosure duties; reimplementing the published algorithm ideas does not. “Pimsleur” and “Michel Thomas Method” are registered trademarks (Justia: PIMSLEUR; michelthomas.com). “Inspired by the Leitner system” is fine; those names must not become Kartky’s own feature branding.

The CEFR is open as a set of categories and levels, but not as a seal of approval. The Council of Europe owns copyright in the CEFR text; its web content may be reproduced for education with credit and no fee, and other uses need permission (CoE; CoE copyright policy). It also states that “it is not the role of the Council of Europe to verify and validate” claimed links to its levels (CoE; CoE further material). Kartky can label decks A1–A2, B1–B2 and C1–C2, described as its own estimate, never “certified” or “officially aligned”.

03Retrieval plus spacing is the proven core Kartky lacks

Kartky lets a learner flip a card and mark it “I know it” or “Still learning”. Nothing brings a word back at the right time. That is the gap, because spacing and retrieval are the best-replicated findings in learning science.

Cepeda and colleagues reviewed 839 assessments from 317 experiments: spacing helps, and the best gap between sessions grows with how long the learner needs to remember (PubMed). For second languages, Kim & Webb’s meta-analysis (Language Learning, 2022) pooled 98 effect sizes from 48 experiments (N = 3,411). Spacing had a medium-to-large effect; short gaps matched long gaps on immediate tests but lost on delayed tests; and equal and expanding schedules were statistically equivalent (Wiley; Western University). Exact effect sizes sit behind the paywall, and a later review cautions that some sub-analyses rest on few studies (SSLLT). The practical reading holds: spacing at all, with enough total lag, matters far more than the exact interval curve.

Retrieval is the second pillar. Karpicke & Roediger (Science, 2008) had students learn 40 Swahili–English pairs. Once an item had been recalled, repeated testing produced about 80% one-week retention; repeated study alone about 36%; dropping the item about 33%. Students’ own predictions did not track their results (Science PDF; Purdue; percentages via a secondary handout). Soderstrom, Kerr & Bjork later showed part of re-study’s poor showing came from spacing confounds; with those controlled, testing still helped more (Soderstrom et al.).

A flip only becomes retrieval practice if the learner genuinely tries to recall before revealing. The interface should ask for that pause.

How many retrievals? Nakata (2017) gave 98 learners 1, 3, 5 or 7 retrievals per word in a session. Five and seven beat one and three, even on a test more than two weeks later, though per minute spent a single retrieval was most efficient (Cambridge Core). His thesis found about five spaced retrievals most desirable, with benefits lasting at least four weeks; massed practice gave the best in-session scores and the worst post-test scores (Nakata thesis abstract). So spread retrievals across days, and don’t let a single first-sight “I know it” retire a card for good.

Direction matters because practice transfers to the direction practised: productive practice wins on productive tests, receptive on receptive (Steinel, Hulstijn & Steinel), and receptive knowledge outruns productive, more so for rarer words (Webb, IRAL). L2→Ukrainian (see “deadline”, recall “крайній термін”) builds recognition; Ukrainian→L2 builds production and spelling. Kartky’s flips run one way only; offering both, each as its own scheduled card, is the cheapest way to cover the productive half of word knowledge.

The broader evidence supports Kartky’s basic design. Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara’s meta-analysis found flashcards and word lists produced “relatively large gains”, larger than writing or fill-in-the-blank tasks (Frontiers 2022). Incidental learning from reading and listening yields only 9–18% of target words immediately and 6–17% after a delay (Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa 2023). Elgort (2011) showed card-learned words produce automatic priming, the signature of real lexical integration (Elgort 2011). A synthesis of 32 word-card studies found ready-made cards produced large effects more often than self-made ones (44% vs 20% of studies), which favours curated decks, provided learners are shown how to use them (Frontiers 2022).

Kartky also covers only one of Nation’s Four Strands. His model gives roughly equal time to meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning and fluency development (SWU journal), and a flashcard app sits almost entirely in the language-focused quarter. The Involvement Load research points to cheap bridges: a meta-analysis of 42 studies found “evaluation” (using or comparing a word in context) carries the most weight, “need” a little, and “search” seems dispensable, though the model explains a modest share of variance (Yanagisawa & Webb 2021; Liu & Reynolds 2022). Three features follow: an ungraded “write your own sentence” prompt, a “pick the right word” choice between near-synonyms, and a timed review of known words for fluency.

04FSRS gives a no-backend app a best-in-class open scheduler

With spaced retrieval settled as the method, the remaining choice is the algorithm. FSRS (Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler) is a community-driven open-source project that Anki adopted in version 23.10 in November 2023.

FSRS models three quantities per item: retrievability, the current probability of recall; stability, the days for recall to fall from 100% to 90%; and difficulty, which governs how fast stability grows (ABC of FSRS; Anki changelog). In the open srs-benchmark, covering about 10,000 Anki collections and roughly 350 million evaluated reviews, FSRS predicts recall more accurately than every other open alternative (srs-benchmark).

The FSRS project claims users need 20–30% fewer reviews than with SM-2 for the same retention, even on default parameters (ABC of FSRS). These are the project’s own simulations, not independent trials. What tips the decision is practical: ts-fsrs is MIT-licensed, its current stable release is 5.4.2 (1 September 2026; a 6.0 beta is in progress), and it ships a UMD bundle that loads with a plain <script> tag, which suits Kartky’s build-free vanilla JavaScript (npm registry; GitHub). Its API is small: createEmptyCard() starts a card, fsrs().next(card, now, Rating.Good) returns the updated card and a log entry, and get_retrievability() orders the queue. Defaults: desired retention 0.9, learning steps of 1 and 10 minutes, and fuzz on so cards learned together don’t all fall due the same day (README).

Defaults suit a no-account site. Optimising FSRS to an individual needs at least “a few hundred” reviews; below that the defaults are used anyway (Anki manual). Anki’s manual also supplies the backlog rules: keep desired retention at 90%, since workload climbs steeply above it; when reviews pile up, stop introducing new cards; and review in order of ascending retrievability so the cards most at risk come first. Because FSRS uses the actual elapsed time at the next review, a learner who skips a week needs no special catch-up logic.

Storage is not a constraint. 18 decks × 50 words = 900 words; two directions make 1,800 cards. At roughly 100 bytes of JSON per card, the whole collection takes a couple of hundred kilobytes of localStorage. Review logs are the only part that grows without limit, so keep only the last few reviews per card.

05Topic decks work when frequency picks the words and themes group them

Organising decks by topic and level is well supported as an idea. It needs care in how words are chosen and sequenced.

The CEFR itself contains no word lists. It provides four domains of language use (personal, public, occupational, educational) and inherits its thematic content from the Council of Europe’s Threshold 1990 (ECML). Semantic sets cause confusion; scenarios don’t

Since Tinkham (1993), studies have found that presenting semantically close words together (kinship terms, colours, foods) increases the trials needed to learn them and produces confusion errors (Tinkham; Frontiers 2022 review). Erten & Tekin (2008) taught 60 children 80 words and found unrelated sets retained better (ÇOMÜ). The best recent study, Nakata & Suzuki (2019, open access), is more balanced: related words produced more interference errors during learning (8.22% vs 2.69%, d = 0.86), yet final scores did not differ significantly (delayed: 21.95% vs 22.54%). Their mini-meta-analysis found related sets need more trials (d = 0.73) but have no significant retention effect, and spacing cut related-word interference errors from 6.32% to 3.70% (Nakata & Suzuki 2019). Thematic clusters, words tied by a scenario rather than a category, help or at least don’t hurt (on Tinkham 1997), and story context may offset most of the relatedness penalty (Bolger & Zapata 2011).

This applies to Kartky’s data today. The English family-beginner deck opens with family, mother, father, parents, mom: five near-neighbours in a row, two of them near-synonyms. The fix is not to drop topics but to turn each deck from a category into a scenario (a family dinner, a birthday call, an airport check-in), mix nouns with verbs, adjectives and phrases, never place co-hyponyms, synonyms or antonym pairs side by side, and let spacing clear the rest. Easily confused pairs such as Spanish suegro/yerno can carry a “commonly confused with” note.

Frequency should decide which words enter each deck

Nation’s coverage study shows the first 2,000 word families cover about 83–89% of running text; comfortable unassisted reading needs about 98% coverage, which means 8,000–9,000 families for writing and 6,000–7,000 for speech (Nation 2006). Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski place the minimum at 4,000–5,000 families for about 95% (RFL). Nation also found that treating recurring topic words as known shifts coverage by “well under 0.5%”: general high-frequency words carry coverage, not topic vocabulary. Researchers map size to CEFR levels very differently, so treat these as rough guides:

Three topics × three bands × 50 words is 450 words per language, about half of Nation’s A2 core. Beginner decks should draw almost entirely on the first 1,000–2,000 words, intermediate decks mostly on 2,000–5,000, and advanced decks on topical and idiomatic items beyond 5,000. Nation’s own model is this hybrid: A1 is a topic-based survival list, and from B2 upward he pairs general high-frequency vocabulary with topic-specific words. No study directly tests “highest-frequency-within-topic” selection, so this pipeline is an inference from coverage and clustering research.

The 50-word set is fine as a container, not as a unit to drill to mastery. With spacing held equal, studying 20 items whole or in parts of 4 or 10 made little difference (Nakata & Webb 2016). One large spaced stack beat several small massed stacks for 90% of learners, while 72% believed the opposite (Kornell 2009). No research supports a specific number of new words per day; the popular “8–10 per lesson” has no traceable source (Conti). A daily new-card limit should be a user setting, offered as a workload control.

Notes on the key sources

  • CEFR-J is the only verified English source that is both level-tagged and licensed for commercial redistribution. It should be Kartky’s reference for A1–B2 English, with Octanove covering C1–C2 (olp-en-cefrj).
  • Spanish has no open CEFR-graded list. Bands will come from wordfreq or FrequencyWords ranks plus Kartky’s own judgement, with the PCIC consulted by hand to spot-check. wordfreq requires credits to Google Books, OpenSubtitles and SUBTLEX, and its data is frozen at about 2021 (wordfreq).
  • Ukrainian lexical data is the weakest link. Wikidata has about 16,000 Ukrainian lexemes but only 371 Ukrainian senses (Wikidata statistics), and VESUM is non-commercial. Ukrainian translations will mostly be Kartky’s own work, which strengthens its claim to them.
  • Tatoeba is the natural source of extra sentences. CC BY 2.0 FR requires attribution but not share-alike, and each sentence has a stable ID linking to its page and author. Store the ID beside any borrowed sentence and generate the credit automatically.
  • Audio is already in good shape. The pronounce button uses the browser’s speechSynthesis, so nothing is redistributed. Voice quality and Ukrainian availability vary by device. If Kartky ever pre-renders audio, check each Piper voice’s model card (Piper VOICES).

AI-written content and share-alike mechanics

The US Copyright Office’s January 2025 report concluded that prompting alone is not authorship; human selection, arrangement and modification can be protected (Copyright Alliance). Anthropic’s commercial terms assign any rights in outputs to the customer (Anthropic). Ukraine’s 2022 law instead creates a separate 25-year sui generis right for non-original computer-generated objects (Art. 33). Human review of every AI-drafted sentence and translation is both a quality requirement and the basis of any ownership claim.

Share-alike applies to adaptations, not to collections. “CC licenses do not require the collection or the compilation itself to be made available under an SA license”, and credit can be given via a linked attribution page (CC FAQ). An unmodified CC BY-SA item stored next to Kartky’s own text (an NGSL rank, an OpenMoji emoji) does not make the deck CC BY-SA; an edited item (a reworded Wiktionary definition) does carry the obligation. Whether one JSON file interleaving both counts as a collection is unsettled, so keep share-alike material in clearly labelled separate fields.

07Legal pitfalls concentrate in content, not in method

The biggest trap is one most developers never hear of: the sui generis database right.

Under the EU Database Directive, a maker who made a “substantial investment” in obtaining, verifying or presenting a database can block extraction or reuse of a substantial part for 15 years (EUR-Lex 96/9/EC). Ukraine adopted the same right in 2023 (Art. 21(4)–(8) of Law 2811-IX); its term restarts whenever contents change substantially, and it forbids “repeated and systematic extraction” of insubstantial parts (zakon.rada.gov.ua). Spain transposes the directive. The US has no such right: after Feist, a frequency-ranked list is thin or unprotected there (Justia), though site terms can still bind scrapers. Kartky’s audience is in Ukraine and Spain, so the EU/Ukrainian rule is the binding one.

A single word, or a single word’s CEFR level, is a fact. Copying the Oxford 3000/5000 or the English Vocabulary Profile wholesale, with their CEFR tags, is the risky act.

OUP licenses its lists commercially (License Global), and Cambridge’s general terms forbid reproduction without written permission (Cambridge terms). The same caution applies to the Instituto Cervantes inventories, Threshold’s word lists and shared Quizlet or Memrise decks; Quizlet’s “copy set” is a permission inside the platform, not a licence to export (Quizlet Help).

08A concrete build plan for Kartky

The plan is ordered so the largest learning gain comes first, and every step stays free, browser-only and account-free.

The core idea

Scheduling first, content second. FSRS in both directions is the change with the biggest payoff and also the cheapest: one MIT-licensed script, a few fields per card and a new localStorage key.

Scheduling core

Phase 13 items
  1. Scheduler

    Vendor the ts-fsrs 5.4.2 UMD bundle with its MIT notice. Give each word a stable ID instead of keying progress by the word string. Create two cards per word: L2→UA first, UA→L2 unlocked after the first successful review. Store FSRS state under a new kartky:v2 key with retention 0.9, steps 1 min / 10 min, fuzz on.

    Why: Spacing and retrieval have the largest effects; FSRS is the most accurate open scheduler; practice transfers to the direction practised

  2. Queues

    One “Review due” queue across all started decks, sorted by ascending retrievability, with a daily cap. Keep “Learn new” per topic × level, with a user-set daily limit (default 10–20). Pause new cards when the backlog is large.

    Why: Anki/FSRS backlog guidance; no evidence-based new-word limit exists, so it is a workload setting

  3. Grading

    Replace “Still learning / I know it” with Again / Good (optionally Hard / Easy) and prompt “think of the answer, then tap”. Treat a first-sight “I know it” as Easy, which still schedules a check. Migrate v1 “known” words as new cards with an early check rather than inventing stability values.

    Why: About five spaced retrievals is optimal; learners misjudge their own learning

Deck content

Phase 23 items
  1. Selection

    Rebuild each deck as scenario sub-groups mixing parts of speech. Check every word against CEFR-J/Octanove (EN) or wordfreq ranks (ES): beginner from the first 1–2k, intermediate 2–5k, advanced 5k+ topical and idiomatic. Shuffle so co-hyponyms and synonyms are never adjacent.

    Why: Frequency drives coverage; semantic clustering causes interference; spacing reduces it

  2. Card fields

    Add a collocations line, Spanish gender/article, verb patterns (“depend on”), register and region tags, “commonly confused with”, and pronunciation notes for Ukrainian speakers (e.g. /θ/). Rewrite the three examples so they differ in structure and collocate.

    Why: Collocation instruction d = 1.415; varied informative contexts; Nation’s word-knowledge aspects

  3. Provenance

    Add a per-word provenance field and a Credits & licences page. Label levels “our estimate”. License Kartky’s own content openly, e.g. CC BY 4.0.

    Why: Attribution mechanics; the Council of Europe does not validate CEFR claims

Practice modes

Phase 32 items
  1. Recall

    Typed UA→L2 recall with accent-tolerant matching (“almost: canción”); multiple-choice recognition on a brand-new word’s first exposure; cloze from the example sentences; complete-the-chunk (“make a ___”).

    Why: Productive recall builds spelling; recognition helps form–meaning early; collocations need explicit practice

  2. Other strands

    Ungraded “write your own sentence”; timed review of known words; optional learner-written memory hook for stubborn words.

    Why: “Evaluation” tasks carry the most weight; fluency strand; keyword effects are short-lived, so keep them optional

Growth

Phase 42 items
  1. Topics

    Add topics from the Threshold / ZNO / PCIC overlap: house and home, health, shopping, work, free time, weather.

    Why: Open theme taxonomies that Ukrainian learners already recognise

  2. Content

    Pull extra sentences from Tatoeba with stored IDs. Optionally add Twemoji images on the answer side for abstract words.

    Why: CC BY sentences with automatic credit; pictures help abstract words most

Throughout

  • A short, skippable “how to study with cards” explainer; cards work best when learners are taught to use them.
  • Show progress as words retained, not days used. Any streak should be forgiving (e.g. 5 of 7 days) with no loss framing.
  • Before adding speech scoring, ML distractors, placement tests or image recommendation, self-search Google Patents.

Harder sessions are the point, not a defect. Bjork’s “desirable difficulties” (spacing, interleaving, retrieval, generation) lower in-session performance while raising long-term retention, and learners still prefer the easier, less effective conditions (APS Observer). One line in the interface, such as “Forgetting a little between reviews makes memory stronger”, keeps a learner from reading a hard review as failure. A Northwestern working paper found streak messaging raised platform use among 60,000 students and that loss aversion drives it, but the causal evidence is thin and the mechanism is easy to abuse (IPR WP-26-05). An FSRS “due today” count is a learning-aligned nudge that comes free with the scheduler.

09Conclusion

The “open methodology” Kartky is looking for is not a single named system to adopt. It is the public research record on spaced retrieval, plus an open-source scheduler that puts it into practice, and neither copyright nor patent law lets anyone fence that off in Ukraine or the EU.

The asset Kartky can build, and own, is a content layer that is frequency-checked, scenario-grouped, collocation-rich and provenance-tracked for Ukrainian speakers.

That layer is exactly where the legal risk sits, and it is also where competitors relying on proprietary lists are weakest. Two findings change what a topic-based app should be. First, the topic is a good way to package and motivate study but a weak way to choose words and a risky way to sequence them. Second, the change with the largest learning payoff, FSRS scheduling in both directions, is also the cheapest to build. The remaining uncertainties (exact meta-analytic effect sizes, Oxford and EVP licensing, the lack of an open CEFR-graded Spanish list, Ukrainian voice quality in browsers) are real but bounded, and none of them blocks Phase 1.
